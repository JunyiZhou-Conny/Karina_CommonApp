SAT Math Notes — Statistical Inference
Copy this whole note to students. Digital SAT Math tests this on almost every exam.

1. Population vs. sample

Population = the entire group you want to learn about.
Example: all students in the United States.

Sample = the smaller part you actually survey or test.
Example: 500 students you surveyed.

Soup analogy:
You are cooking a huge pot of soup and want to know if it needs salt.
You do not drink the whole pot (population).
You take one spoonful (sample).
If that spoonful is salty, you assume the whole pot is salty.

2. When can we trust the sample?

Only if it was randomly selected.
That means every person in the population had an equal chance of being picked.

If you only ask your friends, the sample is biased (unfair).
A website poll, a call-in, or “whoever showed up” is not random.

Key takeaway:
Random sampling is the requirement.
If the sample is not random, you cannot make a reliable claim about the whole population.

3. Making an inference (the smart guess)

An inference is a conclusion from the data.
SAT often asks you to estimate a total from a sample percent.

How:
1) Find the sample proportion (the fraction that meets the condition).
2) Multiply that proportion by the population size.

Example:
A school has 2,000 students.
You survey 200 randomly selected students.
40 of them say blue is their favorite color.
How many students in the whole school likely prefer blue?

Step 1: 40/200 = 0.20 (20%)
Step 2: 0.20 × 2,000 = 400

Inference: about 400 students in the school prefer blue.

Trap:
If the survey only asked seniors, you can only talk about seniors — not the whole school.
The conclusion can only be about the group the sample was pulled from (the sampling frame).
A table that later shows only a subset does not shrink that frame.

Another trap:
Percents that add to 97 or 98 (instead of 100) do NOT kill the poll.
“Not a random sample” is the real problem.

4. Margin of error

Even a good random sample can be a little off from the whole population.
That wiggle room is the margin of error.

45% ± 3% means the true population value is likely between
42% (45 − 3) and 48% (45 + 3).

SAT will not ask you to compute the formula.
They ask you to read what the interval means,
or how to make the margin of error smaller.

Golden rule of sample size:
Larger sample → smaller margin of error (more precise).
Smaller sample → larger margin of error (less precise).

Soup again:
A tiny sip might miss a noodle.
A bigger bowl is a better picture of the pot.
More data → more confidence → smaller wiggle room.

If two random samples come from the same population
and one has a larger margin of error,
that sample was almost certainly the smaller one.
Not because it had a higher percent “in favor.”
Not because it was the larger sample. That is the opposite.

5. Evaluating statistical claims

The SAT asks whether a conclusion is appropriate. Check two things.

1) Was the sample random?
If yes, you may generalize to the population it was drawn from.
If no, stop. The claim about “everyone” is not justified.

2) Was there random assignment?
This is for experiments.
Researchers randomly put people into groups
(for example, fertilizer A vs fertilizer B, or medicine vs sugar pill).
Then they may claim the treatment caused the change.

No random assignment → you may say there is a connection.
You may NOT say “this caused that.”

Watching who already eats breakfast, already exercises, or already uses a product
is an observational study. No cause.

Trick example:
100 volunteers who chose to exercise every day had lower stress.
Can we say exercise causes lower stress for everyone?

No.
They were volunteers (not a random sample of everyone),
and they chose to exercise (not randomly assigned).
You cannot prove cause, and you cannot apply it to everyone.

Key takeaway:
Cause-and-effect needs an experiment with random assignment.

6. SAT checklist (run this on every stats item)

1. Is the sample random?
   If no, the study is biased. You cannot talk about the whole population.
2. Who is the population?
   The conclusion can only be about the group the sample was pulled from.
3. Is there a margin of error?
   That makes a range: sample value ± error.
   “Exactly 52% of all voters” is never justified.
4. How do I make the error smaller?
   Get a bigger random sample.
   A larger margin of error means a smaller sample, not a larger one.
5. Is it an experiment?
   Only random assignment lets you say “this caused that.”
   A survey that just watches is observational.

Pro tip:
“Most likely” value → usually the sample percent or sample mean.
“Range of likely values” → that value plus and minus the margin of error.
